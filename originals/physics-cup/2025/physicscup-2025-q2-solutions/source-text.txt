Problem 2 –  Tetrahedral flock of birds
In total, 256 solutions were submitted for problem 2, of which 111 were correct. Congratulations to everyone who managed to solve it!
There were 61 correct solutions submitted before the first hint was published, and only those were considered as candidates for the best solution awards. Additionally, since there were so many well-written solutions to choose from, the 17 handwritten solutions were not considered this time, sorry about that!
All the solutions can be divided into three categories: (a) those that parameterized the problem through the side lengths of the cuboid defined by the diagonals AB and CD of its opposite faces (or almost equivalent variations of this); (b) those that parameterized the problem through the side lengths of the tetrahedron AB and AC; (c) brute force approach integrating the set of 12 differential equations, either simplifying it by using symmetry, or solving it as is. Note that the hints suggested approach (b).
In each category, the quality of the solution was assessed using the following criteria:
clarity
, i.e. how easy it is for a reader to follow the solution (useful figures increased the rating in this category);
simplicity
: solutions that involved simpler math had higher rating;
rigor
, i.e. any important aspects were left out from the discussions (typos decreased this rating, but very obvious and simple typos were overlooked;
insight
: if there were useful discussions of the analysis and results that went beyond what was asked explicitly.
In category (a), the best solutions were the ones submitted by
Bruno Machado Feltran
(0.4 pts) and by
Alexandru Bordei
(0.15 pts). In category (b), the best solution was submitted by
Eric Wang
(0.3 pts). In category (c), the best solution was submitted jointly by
Adrian Serrano Capatina, Bruno Pontecorvo, and Daniel Gonzalez
Filipov
(0.15 pts).
The reason a brute force approach got awarded this time is that this particular brute-force solution has its own beauty and additional benefits: it utilizes symmetry to simplify calculations, and enables the analytical determination of bird trajectories. It could have been even better if the symmetry had been exploited fully and if there were no inaccuracies, as explained below. The solution starts with putting the starting point of the birds A, B, C, and D, respectively, to (1,1,1), (1,-1,-1), (-1,1,-1), and (-1,-1,1). They notice that if we rotate this system around the
y
-axis, mirror in the
x-z
plane (mirroring was dropped out from the solution), and re-label cyclically the birds, the initial situation is restored. Hence,
x_A=z_D;\; x_B=z_A;\; x_c=z_B;\;x_D=z_C
, for any subsequent moment of time. They also notice that if this symmetry transformation is applied twice, we can conclude that
y_A=y_c;\; y_B=y_D.
However, they fail to notice that the symmetry allows us to establish several additional equalities: everything can be described by three functions of time:
y_A=y_C=-y_B=-y_D\equiv \eta(t);
x_A=z_D=-x_C=-z_B\equiv \xi(t);
x_B=z_A=-x_D=-z_C\equiv \xi(t).
Instead, they derive the additional equalities in a more complicated way.
There are additional excellent solutions published here for various reasons. First, type-(a) solutions by
Robertas Juknevičius
(nice induction-based proof that the birds will remain forming a cuboid),
Karlo Maksimović
(python-generated plot of trajectories),
Ilan Mihelja with Ivan Hegedić
(efficient use of cylindrical coordinates and professional calculation of the integral),
Teo Kai Wen
(very good discussion of symmetries and simulations with a
movie
). Second, type-(b) solutions by
Kanishk Jain
(clean and efficient solution with the only downside that it is not shown how the differential equation is solved);
Zander Li
(clean solution with clear figure and full step-by-step mathematical calculations);
Gleb Grachev
(clean solution with full step-by-step mathematical calculations);
Enej Jauk
(clean solution with useful figures and graphs).
